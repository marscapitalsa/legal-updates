Business introducers need defined boundaries and evidenced oversight

What changed: SAM and PwC published a practical checklist for onboarding and controlling business introducers. What the IAM should do: review existing and new introducers against documented requirements for role boundaries, remuneration, AML responsibilities, client communications, data protection, cross-border activity, monitoring and termination.

What matters

  • Determine whether the third party merely introduces or also solicits, recommends, negotiates, gathers KYC documents, communicates instructions or presents clients as pre-approved.
  • Map the introducer’s place of establishment and activity, target countries, ownership, regulatory status, remuneration and sub-introducers.
  • Document who owns AML checks, client communication, data transfer, conflicts, complaints, record retention and termination.
If the IAM pays the introducer: the IAM should read and apply this page. Record who is paid, the bank account and beneficial recipient, the calculation basis, invoice or supporting record, approval, applicable client disclosure/conflict analysis and whether the payment creates incentives to introduce unsuitable or higher-risk clients. Payment does not by itself determine whether the introducer performs a regulated activity: actual conduct does. A person who only makes a neutral introduction should not advise, recommend products, negotiate the mandate, accept orders, handle money or present the client as approved unless those activities have been separately analysed and authorised.

Why it matters and what may need to be done

  • Before appointment: business sponsor should submit a due-diligence file; compliance should assess reputation, ownership, sanctions, licensing/permitted activity, cross-border restrictions, data protection and conflicts; authorised management should approve the risk and commercial terms.
  • Contract boundaries: prohibit investment advice, binding statements, receipt or transmission of orders, handling client money, delegation and marketing outside approved territories unless separately assessed and authorised. State that the IAM alone accepts clients and sets risk classifications.
  • Remuneration paid by the IAM: specify the fee type and calculation, payer, contractual recipient, beneficial recipient, invoicing evidence, payment account and approval; identify conflict or client-disclosure requirements; prohibit cash or payment to undisclosed third parties and avoid incentives based solely on client volume, AuM or higher-risk business.
  • Introduced clients: apply the IAM’s own KYC, beneficial-owner, source-of-wealth/funds, suitability and sanctions checks. Documents supplied by the introducer should be independently verified and communications retained.
  • Monitoring: track introduced-client count and AuM, acceptance/rejection rates, risk classifications, documentation defects, complaints, sanctions alerts and common banks/structures. Recurrent clustering or poor files should trigger review, suspension or termination.
  • Periodic control: obtain an attestation, refresh sanctions/reputation/licensing checks, review samples of actual communications and confirm the introducer has not expanded countries, staff or sub-introducers without approval.
Practical evidence: due-diligence memorandum; legal/cross-border assessment; approval minutes; signed agreement; remuneration/conflict record; introduced-client register; monitoring dashboard; periodic attestation and sample review.
