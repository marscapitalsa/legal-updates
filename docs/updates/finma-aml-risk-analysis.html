FINMA Guidance 04/2026 · High relevance

AML risk analysis: measurable controls for an IAM

What changed: FINMA’s Guidance 04/2026 says many AML risk analyses still fail to connect the business model, risk tolerance, quantitative limits and control effectiveness. What the IAM should do: update the institution-wide analysis so foreign and Russia-linked clients, introducer-sourced business, sanctions exposure and outsourced controls are measured, approved and monitored rather than merely described.

Executive action points

  • Confirm the approved risk tolerance: locate the board-approved AML risk analysis and check whether it expressly addresses Russia-linked relationships, introducer-sourced clients, complex structures, sanctions exposure and prohibited business.
  • Confirm quantitative limits: if the methodology does not yet contain approved limits, define them for both number of relationships and AuM, in absolute and percentage terms.
  • Reassess Russian-linked clients individually: distinguish nationality, residence, source of wealth, source of funds, beneficial ownership, operating interests, banks and transaction routes.
  • Review each introducer: identify its exact jurisdiction, regulatory status, ownership, remuneration, client-selection process and actual role; a broad regional description is not sufficient for geographic risk assessment.
  • Define exceptions: relationships outside risk tolerance require a restrictive, centrally recorded exception-to-policy (ETP) process; exceptions cannot become a permanent way to exceed a portfolio limit.
  • Control outsourced compliance: require data, testing results and effectiveness metrics; a generic annual compliance confirmation is not enough.

Why it matters and what may need to be done

The risk analysis is a management instrument, not a descriptive appendix to the AML policy. The governing body should approve the risk appetite and exclusions; management should translate them into measurable limits, allocate resources and act on breaches; compliance should define and test controls; the client owner should collect and update the underlying facts. Outsourcing compliance does not outsource these decisions or accountability.

Is a Russian client automatically high risk?

No single label should be assigned solely from the fact that a client is Russian. FATF’s suspension of the Russian Federation is distinct from FATF’s public grey- and black-list process. At the same time, Switzerland maintains extensive Russia-related sanctions, so a Russian nexus is a material geographic, sanctions and transaction-risk factor that must be reflected in the IAM’s own methodology.

A lower-risk outcome may be supportable where the client is not a PEP, beneficial ownership is simple and verified, wealth is transparently generated and independently corroborated, funds come through reputable non-sanctioned banks, expected transactions are consistent and sanctions screening shows no ownership/control exposure. Risk rises where there are opaque offshore structures, proximity to state-controlled sectors or sanctioned persons, unexplained wealth, frequent changes of bank or domicile, third-party funding, complex payment chains, high-risk intermediaries or transactions inconsistent with the profile.

Required client-by-client Russia nexus assessment

  • Identity and nexus: nationality, tax residence, physical residence, former residence, citizenship changes and significant economic interests.
  • Beneficial ownership: controlling persons, family members, trusts, foundations, holding companies and nominee arrangements.
  • Source of wealth: business or employment history, sectors, counterparties, sale events, inheritance and independent corroboration.
  • Source of funds: remitting account, bank jurisdiction, account holder, payment purpose and consistency with known wealth.
  • Sanctions: client and connected persons, entities owned or controlled by listed persons, banks, issuers and transaction counterparties.
  • Expected activity: currencies, countries, transaction types, expected annual flows and reasons for deviations.
  • Introducer influence: whether the introducer supplies documents, communicates for the client, recommends structures or participates in payments.

Illustrative quantitative limits

FINMA does not prescribe a universal percentage for Russian clients. The board must select limits consistent with the IAM’s strategy, controls and resources. The following is an example architecture—not a regulatory safe harbour and not a recommendation to adopt the figures without reviewing the current client book.

  • Zero tolerance: sanctioned persons/entities; prohibited services; unverifiable beneficial owner; materially unexplained source of wealth/funds; knowingly misleading KYC information.
  • Russia-nexus portfolio limit: for example, an early-warning threshold at 7.5% and a hard limit at 10% of both client relationships and total AuM. Use separate figures because a low client count can still represent a high AuM concentration.
  • Elevated-risk relationships: for example, warning at 10% and hard limit at 15% of relationships/AuM across all elevated-risk categories. Do not combine low-, medium- and high-risk relationships into one number.
  • Introducer concentration: for example, no single introducer to account for more than 20% of new clients in a rolling 12-month period without management review; a lower threshold may be appropriate if clients share a geographic or sanctions nexus.
  • ETP limit: preferably zero for prohibited criteria. For other exclusions, set a very low numerical cap and require executive-board approval, central recording and board oversight. An ETP must not authorise a general breach of a portfolio limit.
  • Control-quality indicator: target 100% current KYC/high-risk reviews and 0 overdue sanctions alerts; separately report documentation deficiencies found in compliance samples.
Critical caveat: before selecting percentages, calculate the current number and AuM of Russian-linked, introducer-sourced and other elevated-risk relationships. A limit below the current exposure immediately creates a breach requiring a documented remediation plan; setting a limit just above current exposure without analysis would not demonstrate genuine risk tolerance.

How to structure the risk analysis

For each relevant criterion, show inherent risk, the specific mitigating controls, control effectiveness and resulting net risk separately. Do not lower inherent risk because controls exist; controls belong in the control-risk assessment. FINMA notes that very high inherent risk cannot be reduced below high net risk even by effective controls.

  • Cover client segments, domicile/residence, products and services even where risk is assessed as low or medium.
  • Separate countries and client types where their criticality differs; do not group Russia with low-risk jurisdictions.
  • Describe actual controls or refer to a precise control-inventory item—not merely “according to the AML policy”.
  • Use effectiveness evidence: sample size, findings, overdue reviews, unresolved alerts and remediation time.
  • Compare individual and aggregate net risk with risk tolerance and define mandatory action for a breach.

Business introducer controls

The introducer’s exact country and place of activity must be recorded. The IAM should not delegate client acceptance, beneficial-owner identification, source-of-wealth assessment or sanctions decisions to the introducer.

  • Perform onboarding due diligence on the introducer, its owners, management, reputation, regulatory status and sanctions exposure.
  • Define in writing that the introducer makes introductions only and cannot bind the IAM or present a client as pre-approved.
  • Map remuneration and disclose/manage conflicts where necessary.
  • Track risk and acceptance rates of introduced clients separately from direct clients.
  • Trigger review for recurring incomplete documents, similar structures, the same banks/intermediaries or unexplained client clustering.

Outsourced compliance: minimum oversight

The IAM remains accountable. Management should receive a periodic pack showing at least the number and AuM of elevated-risk and Russia-linked relationships, sanctions alerts, overdue reviews, ETPs, introducer-sourced clients, sample-testing results and open remediation actions.

  • Monthly or quarterly: KRI report depending on activity and risk; immediate escalation for sanctions or material KYC issues.
  • Quarterly: management review of limits, breaches, ETPs and remediation.
  • Annually and on material change: refresh the institution-wide AML risk analysis and reassess resources.

These frequencies are proposed internal controls; FINMA’s Guidance requires effectiveness and proportionality but does not prescribe these exact intervals for every IAM.

Evidence and timing expected in practice

  1. At approval and at least on the institution’s scheduled annual refresh: evidence the business model, inherent risks, tolerance, controls, effectiveness and aggregate net-risk conclusion.
  2. On a material change: reassess when a new market, introducer, product, client segment or sanctions exposure changes the risk profile.
  3. At each reporting cycle: show actual exposure against warnings and hard limits by both relationship count and AuM; record breaches, root cause, owner and remediation date.
  4. For every ETP: record the case-specific rationale, executive approval and board oversight; do not use an exception to permit a continuing aggregate-limit breach.
