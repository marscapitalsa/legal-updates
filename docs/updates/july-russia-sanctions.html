July sanctions on Russia: a four-regime exposure review

July brought the EU’s 21st package, new UK Russia designations and cyber measures, and US list and licensing changes. These measures are not automatically identical to Swiss law: a Swiss IAM must first apply the Swiss ordinance, then identify any EU, UK or US nexus and the requirements imposed by banks, custodians and markets.

What changed in July 2026

  • European Union — 23 July: the 21st package added 48 individuals and 170 entities. It introduced transaction bans for 33 additional Russian credit and financial institutions, four non-EU banks and 14 non-EU crypto-related platforms; added 41 shadow-fleet vessels; tightened dual-use controls for 51 entities; and added measures concerning refineries, LNG tankers and the oil-price-cap mechanism.
  • European Union — 17 July: one individual and five entities associated with Russian drone manufacturing were listed.
  • United Kingdom — 13 July: the first joint UK–EU cyber package targeted 24 persons and entities connected with Russian state, cybercriminal and information operations. The UK Russia regime recorded ten new designations on 13 July, amendments/revocation on 7 July, a variation on 14 July, and further variations/amendment on 23 July.
  • United States: OFAC published Russia-related designation updates on 20 July, amended a Russia-related general licence and FAQs on 24 July, and added a Russian industrial entity to the Sectoral Sanctions Identifications List on 27 July.
  • Switzerland: as at 28 July, SECO had not announced Swiss adoption of the EU’s 21st package. The most recent confirmed Swiss package action remained the May adoption of listings from the EU’s 20th package. EU adoption alone does not amend the Swiss sanctions ordinance.

Why it matters and what may need to be done

  • Separate legal applicability: document which regime applies to each relationship or transaction. Swiss sanctions apply to the IAM’s Swiss activity. EU, UK or US rules may additionally apply because of a client, beneficial owner, employee, group entity, bank, custodian, currency-clearing route, security, issuer, platform, service provider or place of performance. A provider’s contractual screening standard may be broader than the law directly binding the IAM.
  • Swiss list rescreening: whenever SECO changes the Swiss annexes, promptly rescreen clients, beneficial owners, authorised persons, related entities, banks, issuers, counterparties and relevant portfolio holdings. Preserve the list/version, query, result, date/time, operator and reviewer.
  • July exposure sweep: independently check whether portfolios, payments or counterparties touch the newly listed banks, crypto platforms, refineries, military-industrial entities or shadow-fleet ecosystem. Screen securities by issuer and ownership/control, not merely by instrument name.
  • Potential match: stop the affected onboarding, trade, transfer or payment pending review; establish identity and ownership/control; determine the applicable prohibition, exemption or licence; involve compliance/legal; and record the release, rejection, freeze or report decision. Do not tell a client how to restructure around a restriction.
  • Russian clients: nationality alone is not a sanctions match or a complete AML risk classification. The file should instead address residence, beneficial ownership, source of wealth/funds, banks, transaction routes, connected persons and entities, business sectors and sanctions ownership/control exposure.
  • Portfolio and product controls: product approval and ongoing monitoring should identify whether a fund, AMC, certificate, custody chain or underlying asset could become untradeable, unpayable or illiquid if a bank, issuer, platform or service provider is designated.
  • Change governance: maintain a sanctions-change register showing publication date, Swiss implementation status, affected relationships/instruments, screening completion, owner and closure. Urgent list changes should not wait for the next periodic client review.

Which sources should be monitored

  • Switzerland: SECO sanctions measures, ordinance amendments, sanctions-address search and EAER/Federal Council announcements.
  • European Union: Council sanctions timeline and press releases, EUR-Lex legal acts, EU Financial Sanctions Database and Commission sanctions FAQs.
  • United Kingdom: UK Sanctions List, Russia notices, OFSI financial-sanctions guidance/FAQs, general licences and enforcement updates.
  • United States: OFAC Recent Actions, SDN and SSI list changes, Russia-related general licences, FAQs and enforcement releases.
Practical evidence: applicability/nexus memorandum; exposure sweep; rescreening log; ownership-and-control analysis; blocked/released transaction decision; provider confirmation; management sanctions-change register.
