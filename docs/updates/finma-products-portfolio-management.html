FINMA Guidance 03/2026 · High relevance

Products in individual portfolio management: what the IAM should do

What changed: FINMA’s Guidance 03/2026 consolidates supervisory expectations drawn from escalation cases involving unsuitable, illiquid, concentrated, proprietary and insufficiently reviewed products in individual mandates. What the IAM should do: before launching or using an AMC or another higher-risk product, document approval, enhanced product due diligence, mandate suitability, liquidity, valuation, conflicts and ongoing monitoring.

Executive action points

  • Before an AMC is launched or first purchased: approve it through a documented product process covering issuer/structurer, supervision, insolvency exposure, valuation, liquidity, costs, strategy governance and target clients.
  • Before allocation to a client: record why the instrument is suitable for that client’s financial capacity, risk appetite, horizon, restrictions and agreed strategy; pay particular attention to retail clients.
  • For foreign funds and illiquid assets: document regulatory equivalence, audited information, redemption/exit mechanics, valuation reliability and portfolio concentration.
  • If the IAM or a related party benefits from the product: identify the conflict, use objective selection criteria, prevent remuneration incentives from favouring the product and disclose unavoidable conflicts.
  • During the holding period: monitor liquidity, valuation, issuer/structurer, audit status, performance and continued suitability; define escalation and exit triggers.
  • If compliance or risk control is outsourced: the mandate must expressly cover these product risks, but accountability and sufficient expertise remain with the IAM. A named internal function must own portfolio-risk monitoring and challenge the provider.

Why it matters and what may need to be done

FINMA’s escalation cases concerned foreign collective investment schemes, AMCs, products issued through structuring companies, in-house or insufficiently supervised products, and complex or illiquid instruments. An IAM with any such exposure should map the affected products and mandates and be able to demonstrate the controls below. FINMA observed substantial client losses where suitability, disclosure, diversification, conflicts, initial due diligence or ongoing controls were inadequate.

Scenario 1 — the IAM issues, sponsors or manages the strategy of an AMC

The portfolio-management or product sponsor should request approval before launch or before the AMC is placed on the approved investment list. Approval should come from a body independent enough to challenge the commercial sponsor—normally the investment/product committee with documented input from risk and compliance, and governing-body escalation where conflicts or exposures are material. The same person should not originate, approve and monitor the product without an evidenced independent challenge. The approval memorandum should identify the legal issuer and structurer, their regulatory status, unsecured issuer exposure and insolvency implications, calculation agent, custody chain, valuation source, liquidity provider, termination events, secondary-market limitations, total cost stack and remuneration received by the IAM or related persons.

  • Define the investment universe, leverage, concentration and liquidity limits of the AMC strategy.
  • Specify the target client and negative target market. A complex or illiquid AMC should not be treated as suitable merely because the mandate permits “alternative investments”.
  • Document whether the IAM considers only the AMC, only own products, or also competing third-party instruments when making allocations.
  • If third-party alternatives exist, record objective comparison criteria such as expected risk/return, liquidity, transparency, issuer risk and all-in costs.
  • Prevent portfolio managers’ compensation from creating an incentive to allocate to the AMC.
  • Prepare client disclosure covering structure, issuer risk, liquidity, valuation, costs, conflicts and the IAM’s role; retain evidence that it was provided before the investment decision where required.
  • Perform enhanced due diligence where the AMC, issuer or structuring vehicle lacks equivalent prudential supervision: review audited financial information, ownership and governance, service providers, valuation controls, hedging/collateral mechanics, legal enforceability and any auditor qualification or change.
Practical evidence: product approval memo; term sheet; issuer/structurer due-diligence file; fee and conflict map; target-market decision; approved-investment-list entry; investment committee minutes; client disclosure; periodic monitoring report.

Scenario 2 — an AMC is purchased for a particular mandate

The client file should show why the allocation fits the complete portfolio, not only why the product is attractive. For a retail client, the suitability note should cover loss-bearing capacity, time horizon, liquidity needs, knowledge/experience, concentration after purchase and the effect of issuer and strategy risks.

  • Set a mandate-level maximum allocation for the AMC or for complex/illiquid products as a category.
  • Escalate an exception before trading; do not document suitability retrospectively.
  • Explain how the product affects diversification and whether correlated exposures exist elsewhere in the portfolio.
  • Provide meaningful performance information, including valuation uncertainty and material fees.

Scenario 3 — foreign funds or other products without equivalent supervision

FINMA identifies missing audited accounts, qualified or outstanding audit opinions, changes or termination of the auditor and unreliable information on structure, valuation or liquidity as risk indicators. The IAM should therefore classify each such product and determine enhanced due diligence proportionate to the risk.

  • Verify the fund, manager, administrator, custodian and auditor and assess the home supervisory framework.
  • Review offering documents, latest audited accounts, side pockets, gates, notice periods, suspension rights and valuation methodology.
  • Define red flags that block a new investment or trigger review: delayed NAV, unavailable audit, auditor resignation, unexplained valuation change, redemption restriction or material related-party transaction.
  • Maintain an exit plan and assess whether the client can bear a delayed or unavailable exit.

Scenario 4 — illiquid assets

For each mandate, the IAM should aggregate all illiquid exposures rather than reviewing each instrument in isolation. The control should compare the resulting percentage with the client’s foreseeable cash needs, investment horizon and agreed restrictions.

  • Define what counts as illiquid and how borderline instruments are treated.
  • Set warning and hard limits by client category or mandate.
  • Use an independent or otherwise challengeable valuation source and document stale-price controls.
  • Stress-test a scenario in which exits are unavailable and liquid assets fall in value simultaneously.

If compliance or risk control is outsourced

The IAM remains responsible under Article 17 FinIO. The outsourcing agreement should cover product governance, conflicts, client disclosures and sample testing. The designated internal risk owner should maintain the inventory of concentration, liquidity, valuation and issuer risks. Responsibilities must not overlap ambiguously.

  • Assign a named internal owner for every control and every outsourced deliverable.
  • Give the provider complete access to product and mandate data.
  • Require IAM-specific testing rather than a generic checklist.
  • Review findings, overdue actions and exceptions at management level.

Evidence and timing expected in practice

  1. Before launch, admission or first purchase: product approval, due diligence, conflict decision, target market and monitoring owner should be documented.
  2. Before each client allocation: suitability, portfolio concentration, liquidity capacity and required disclosure should be evidenced.
  3. While held: review at the risk-based frequency set in the approval; events such as delayed NAV, auditor change, redemption restriction, issuer deterioration or limit breach should trigger an immediate reassessment.
  4. For legacy holdings: gaps should be inventoried and remediated on a risk-prioritised timetable approved and tracked by management.

The timetable above is a proposed implementation plan, not a deadline prescribed by FINMA.
